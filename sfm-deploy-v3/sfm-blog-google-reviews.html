How Do I Get More Google Reviews for My Business?

J
Jayson Hines August 18, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: you have to ask, out loud, right after you finish the job, and then follow up with a text that has your direct review link in it. That's it. Almost every business I look at that has 11 reviews and a competitor down the road with 340 has the same problem. Nobody's asking.

I hear the same thing from owners in Perrysburg and Toledo all the time. "Our customers love us, they just don't leave reviews." They're right about the first part. The second part isn't a customer problem. A happy customer drives away thinking you were great and then goes back to their day. They're never going to open Google on their own and hunt down your listing. You have to hand them the link while they still feel good about you.

Why aren't my customers leaving reviews on their own?

Because leaving a review is work and being happy isn't. Think about the last five businesses you were genuinely pleased with. How many did you review? Probably zero.

The people who review without being asked are usually the angry ones. Frustration is a much stronger motivator than satisfaction, so if you never ask anybody, the only reviews you collect over time are from the handful of customers who had a bad day with you. That's how a good business ends up with a 3.6 rating.

Asking flips that. When you ask everybody, the happy majority finally shows up in your average.

Where do I find my Google review link?

Sign in to your Google Business Profile, either through the dashboard or by searching your own business name while logged into the account that manages it. There's an option to ask for reviews or share a review form. That gives you a short link that drops the customer straight onto the star-rating screen.

Use that link and nothing else. Do not send people to your Google Business Profile page and hope they find the review button. Do not tell them to "look us up on Google." Every extra step you make somebody take cuts your response rate, and the drop is brutal.

Once you have it, put it everywhere. Save it as a text shortcut on your phone. Put it in your email signature. Turn it into a QR code and stick it on the counter, on the invoice, on the back of the truck if you want.

When's the best time to ask for a review?

When they're happiest, which is almost never the same as when it's convenient for you.

For a service business, that's the moment the job is done and they're looking at the finished work. For a restaurant or a shop, it's when they're paying and telling you they'll be back. For anything you deliver, it's a day or two after they've actually used the thing.

What doesn't work is a mass email blast three weeks later to everyone who's ever bought from you. By then the feeling is gone and you're just another email.

The one-two that works best: ask in person, then text the link within the hour. The in-person ask gets the commitment. The text makes it easy enough that they actually follow through. If you only do one, do the text. But doing both together pulls in noticeably more than either one on its own, every time we've set it up for a client.

What do I actually say when I ask?

Keep it short and human. Something like "Hey, if you were happy with how this turned out, would you mind leaving us a quick Google review? I'll text you the link right now, it takes about thirty seconds." Then send it before you leave the driveway.

Two things matter in that. You said it takes thirty seconds, which lowers the perceived effort, and you're sending the link immediately instead of "sometime later."

For the text itself, use their name, mention the specific job, and paste the link. "Hi Karen, thanks again for having us out for the kitchen today. Here's that review link if you get a minute: [link]. Really appreciate it." Nobody needs a paragraph.

Do not send the same generic template to two hundred people at once. Google can tell, customers can tell, and a wall of reviews that all showed up on the same Tuesday looks exactly like what it is.

Can I offer a discount or a gift card for reviews?

No, and this one gets businesses in real trouble. Google's policies prohibit incentivized reviews. If you offer money, a discount, a free drink, or entry into a drawing in exchange for a review, those reviews can get stripped and your profile can take a hit that's a lot harder to undo than it was to cause.

Same goes for review gating, meaning you ask people how their experience was first and only send the Google link to the ones who say "great." That's against the rules too.

You can ask everyone. You just can't pay them.

What do I do about a bad review?

Respond to it, publicly, within a day or two, and don't get defensive. Half the people reading your reviews are reading the responses to see what kind of operator you are. A calm, specific reply to an angry review does more for you than three more five-star ratings.

The formula's boring on purpose. Thank them, acknowledge the specific thing that went wrong, say what you're doing about it, and offer to take it offline with a real name and phone number. No excuses, no arguing about who's right.

And a couple of bad reviews mixed in is fine. A perfect 5.0 across 200 reviews actually makes people suspicious. The sweet spot most shoppers trust is somewhere in the mid to high fours.

How many Google reviews do I actually need?

Enough to beat whoever's showing up above you in the map pack. Go look. Search the thing your customers would search, see who lands in the top three, and count their reviews. That's your number, and it's the only number that means anything for your market.

Reviews also feed how you rank, not just whether people pick you. Google looks at review count, rating, how recently they came in, and what the words in them say. A steady trickle every week beats forty reviews you collected during one big push in March and then nothing. If you want the full picture on the ranking side, I broke it down in how to actually rank on Google Maps.

Start with a goal of two a week. That's boring and it works. Two a week is a hundred a year, and a hundred reviews puts you ahead of most local competitors in Northwest Ohio without doing anything clever.

How do I make this actually happen every week?

Systems, not willpower. If asking for reviews depends on you remembering at the end of a long day, it won't happen past week two.

Pick one person who owns it. Add the ask to your closeout checklist so it's part of the job, not an extra. Set up an automated text that fires when a job gets marked complete in whatever software you use. Then look at the count on the first of every month, because anything you don't measure quietly stops.

Common questions

How do I get my Google review link?
Sign in to the Google account that manages your business, open your Google Business Profile, and use the option to ask for reviews or share a review form. That gives you a short link that takes people straight to the star rating screen. Save it in your phone so you can text it in seconds.

Can I offer a discount or gift card for a Google review?
No. Google's policies prohibit incentivized reviews, so offering money, discounts, free items, or contest entries in exchange for a review can get those reviews removed and hurt your profile. You can ask every customer. You just cannot pay them.

Why did my Google review disappear?
Reviews get filtered when they look incentivized, come from an account with no history, get posted from the same device or network as the business, or arrive in a sudden burst. A steady pace of real reviews from real customers is much less likely to get pulled.

Should I respond to every Google review?
Yes, good and bad. Responses are public and shoppers read them to figure out what you are like to deal with. Keep the good ones short and specific. For the bad ones, thank them, address the actual issue, and offer to take it offline with a name and a phone number.

How do I get a fake or unfair review removed?
Report it through your Google Business Profile as a policy violation and explain why it breaks the rules, for example it is spam, it is from a competitor, or the person was never a customer. Removal is not guaranteed, so respond publicly and calmly in the meantime since that reply is what everyone else sees.
