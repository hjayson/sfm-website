How Do I Get More Google Reviews for My Business?

J
Jayson Hines August 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: ask every happy customer, right when the good moment is fresh, with a direct link that drops them straight into the review box. That's it. No trick, no software subscription required. Most businesses that struggle with reviews aren't getting bad service, they're just not asking, or they're asking wrong.

I've watched this play out with dozens of clients now. The ones with 150+ reviews aren't doing anything fancier than the ones stuck at 12. They're just consistent about the ask, and they make it stupid easy for the customer to follow through.

Here's the full process, plus the stuff that'll get your profile in trouble if you're not careful.

Why bother? Do Google reviews actually matter?

Yes, more than almost anything else on your Google Business Profile. Recent survey data puts the number of consumers who read reviews before choosing a local business somewhere north of 90%, and most of those people are looking specifically at Google, not Yelp or Facebook.

Reviews also feed directly into your local ranking. Google looks at how many reviews you have, how recent they are, and what they say, when it decides which three businesses show up in the map pack for a search like "roofer near me." A thin, stale review page is one of the easiest reasons a competitor with worse work is beating you to the top. If you haven't looked at your Maps ranking lately, I covered that separately in how to actually rank on Google Maps.

When should you ask for a review?

As close to the good moment as you can get. If you just finished a job, sent an invoice, or a customer walked out happy, that's the window. Wait a week and the moment's gone. People don't forget you existed, they just forget the feeling that made them want to say something nice.

For service businesses, ask on-site or in the follow-up text the same day. For retail or restaurants, a receipt link or a QR code at the register catches people while they're still holding the bag. Don't wait for the "perfect" customer either. Ask everyone who had a normal, decent experience. You don't need a five-star superfan, you just need someone who wasn't unhappy. Volume and consistency beat cherry-picking.

What's the easiest way to ask?

Pull your direct review link. Search your business name on Google, open your Business Profile, and there's a button called "Ask for reviews" that generates a shareable link. That link takes the customer straight to the star rating with no searching, no scrolling, no login confusion.

Text it. Email it. Print it as a QR code on receipts, invoices, or a small sign near the register. The fewer steps between "I liked this business" and "review posted," the more reviews you'll get. Every extra tap loses people. Something like this works fine: "Hey Sarah, thanks again for having us out today! If you have 30 seconds, we'd really appreciate a quick Google review: [link]. Means a lot to a small local business like ours."

What will get you in trouble with Google?

Paying for reviews. Offering a discount, a gift card, or an entry into a drawing in exchange for one. Review gating, which is asking happy customers for a public review while quietly routing unhappy ones to a private form so only good ones ever hit Google. All three break Google's policy, and getting caught can mean losing reviews you already earned or having your whole profile suspended.

Ask everyone the same way. Let the reviews land where they land. If someone's unhappy, that's information you need anyway.

How do you handle a bad review?

Respond. Every time, good or bad. A bad review with no response looks like you don't care. A bad review with a calm, specific response, one that acknowledges the issue and says what you did about it, often reads better to a future customer than five perfect reviews in a row. It shows you're a real business run by real people.

Don't argue in the comments. If it needs a real conversation, say so publicly and take it to a phone call or email.

Common questions

How long does it take to see more Google reviews?
Most businesses see new reviews within a week or two of actually asking every customer, since the biggest reason people don't leave one is that nobody asked. Send a direct link right after a good experience and you'll usually see a handful within the first month.

Can I offer a discount or gift card for a Google review?
No. Google's policy bans paying for reviews or offering any incentive tied to leaving one, whether it's cash, a discount, or a giveaway entry. Businesses caught doing this can have reviews removed or their whole profile suspended. Ask for honest feedback instead, with no strings attached.

What should I do about a bad Google review?
Respond to it, calmly and without getting defensive. Acknowledge the issue, say what you're doing about it, and take the details offline if it needs a real conversation. A thoughtful response often builds more trust with future customers than the review itself takes away.

Do older Google reviews matter less over time?
Yes, recency matters. Google and customers both weigh recent reviews more heavily than old ones, so a profile with 80 reviews from three years ago and nothing since looks less trustworthy than one with 20 reviews spread out over the last few months. Consistency beats a big one-time push.

What's the easiest way to share my Google review link?
Pull your direct review link from your Google Business Profile (search your business name, click "Ask for reviews," and copy the link), then text or email it right after a job's done or a customer checks out. A QR code on a receipt, invoice, or counter sign works the same way with even less friction.
