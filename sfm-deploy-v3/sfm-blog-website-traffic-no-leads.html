Why Is My Website Getting Traffic but No Leads?

J
Jayson Hines October 3, 2026 · 6 min read

If your website gets traffic but no leads, check three things first: whether those visitors are potential customers, whether they can easily request help, and whether you are measuring completed inquiries correctly. More visitors will not fix a broken form or an offer that does not match what people came to find.

Social media can get someone interested. Your website then has a job to do: explain the service, earn some trust, and make the next step obvious. For a local service business, that next step might be a phone call, an estimate request, or a booked appointment.

Here is how to find the problem without starting with a complete redesign.

1. Can a customer actually reach you?

Open the page on your phone using the same link a customer would tap in your social profile, ad, or search result. Follow it all the way through. Do not stop when the button looks right.

Send a clearly labeled test inquiry through your own form. Check that the success message appears and the inquiry reaches the correct inbox or customer management system. Check the spam folder too. If the page sends people to a booking calendar, confirm that it loads and has appropriate availability. Use the provider's test process when testing a booking, so you do not block a real appointment.

Tap the phone number and confirm it opens the correct number. Check that any form error tells you what needs fixing. A required field that cannot be completed on a phone can stop an otherwise interested customer.

Fix this first: a broken contact path deserves attention before a new headline, another post, or more ad spend.

2. Are you attracting buyers, or people looking for a quick answer?

Look at the pages receiving visits and the places those visitors come from. A person reading a how-to article may want to solve one problem themselves. A person visiting a service page may be comparing providers. Those visits should not be judged as if they have the same purpose.

For example, someone researching how to merge two Facebook pages is not necessarily shopping for a social media agency. A useful article can still introduce your business, but it needs a relevant next step for readers who want ongoing help.

Separate visits to service pages from visits to informational posts. Check whether the audience is in an area you serve. If most of your visits are unrelated to your services or service area, changing the contact button alone will not solve the mismatch.

Fix this first: connect a useful article to the service that naturally follows from it. Keep the advice helpful even for people who never become customers.

3. Does the landing page deliver what the post promised?

Imagine a local remodeler posts a kitchen renovation and invites people to request an estimate. The link goes to a homepage full of general company information, with no kitchen examples and no obvious estimate request. The visitor now has to find the thing they were just offered.

Send that person to the relevant service page. Repeat the offer clearly, show examples of that work, explain where you work, and provide the promised next step. This is an illustrative example, not a client result.

Read the first screen of your page as if you have never heard of the business. Can you tell what it does, who it helps, and where it operates? Replace vague claims like “solutions for your success” with the actual service and the problem it solves.

Fix this first: choose one important social post or ad and follow its link. Make the destination answer the question that prompted the click.

4. Is the next step clear and reasonable?

“Contact us” may be enough in a navigation menu. Beside a service description, “Request a roof inspection” or “Ask about social media management” tells the visitor more about what will happen.

Explain the next step before asking for personal information. Will someone call to discuss the project? Is this an appointment request or a confirmed booking? Only promise a response time your team can consistently meet.

Ask for the information you need to respond and assess fit. A detailed project questionnaire may make sense later. It can be too much for someone who is only trying to find out whether you offer the service.

Give people enough evidence to make that decision: relevant work examples, accurate service details, and genuine customer feedback used with permission. Avoid claims you cannot support.

If the page itself needs work, our website design for local businesses focuses on making your services clear and giving visitors a practical way to contact you.

5. Does the page work comfortably on a phone?

Use the page yourself on a mobile connection. Watch for text that is difficult to read, a chat widget covering the button, images that move the page while loading, and forms that become awkward when the keyboard opens.

Then run the page through Google's PageSpeed Insights. The report separates real-user data, when available, from a simulated performance test. Google's Core Web Vitals guidance covers loading, responsiveness, and visual stability. Use those findings to identify specific repairs.

A better performance score does not prove that your website will generate more leads. It can help you remove obstacles, which you then need to evaluate alongside actual inquiries.

6. Are you counting an inquiry, or just a click?

A tap on “Book a call” does not tell you whether the person finished booking. A tap on a phone number does not tell you whether a conversation happened. Keep those actions separate in your reports.

Google Analytics recommends the generate_lead event for a submitted form or request for information. Its lead-form reporting guide shows how to examine the steps from a visit to a form start and submission. Your website or booking provider may need additional setup to measure successful completion.

Have the person responsible for tracking verify that a successful inquiry is recorded once, and that an error or an unfinished form is not counted as a completed lead. Compare the report with the inquiries actually received. Consent choices and tracking limitations mean the totals may differ.

Also check what happens after an inquiry arrives. A missed notification or unanswered message is a follow-up problem. Record whether each inquiry was relevant and whether your team responded, instead of treating every submission as a new customer.

What should you change first?

Start with one service page and one path into it. Record the starting date, where visitors come from, completed inquiries, and how many fit your services. Fix a broken inquiry path immediately. Then improve the clearest problem with the message or next step, and keep a note of what changed.

Compare similar periods and account for changes in traffic source or advertising. With only a handful of visits or inquiries, one quiet week is not enough to judge a redesign or promise a conversion rate.

If you are deciding whether to send more traffic through posts or paid campaigns, read our guide to organic social media versus Facebook ads. Get the destination ready before increasing the effort you put into sending people there.

You do not need every page rebuilt to start making progress. You need to know where interested people are getting stuck, fix that step, and check what happens next.
