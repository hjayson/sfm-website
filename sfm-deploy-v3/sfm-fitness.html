HOW WE WORK WITH GYMS

We would rather fix retention
than sell you more leads.

Offers designed to filter, not just to fill

An intro offer should attract someone who fits how you actually coach. That usually means a structured on-ramp or a paid intro rather than a race to the lowest price, because average revenue per member and churn together decide what a member is worth far more than the sign-up number does.

Speed of follow-up, because that is where leads die

A large share of gym leads never get a second contact attempt, and response time is one of the biggest factors in whether someone ever books. We will look at what happens after the form gets submitted before recommending you spend more on getting more forms submitted.

Content aimed at the member who is about to quit

Most gym marketing talks to strangers. Very little of it talks to the member at month three who has started skipping. Member stories, coach check-ins, and community content do real retention work, and retention is worth more to your revenue than another January signup.

Planning around the calendar you already know is coming

January is your best acquisition month and February is when a big share of those people disappear. November and December are usually your worst months for net membership. None of that is a surprise, so it should be in the plan and in the cash forecast rather than something that happens to you every year.

WHY GYMS ARE DIFFERENT

More leads will not save a gym
that leaks members.

Most gym marketing sells the front door. The business is actually won or lost at month three, and that changes what is worth spending money on.

Churn decides your value, not sign-ups

Lifetime value is roughly your average revenue per member divided by your monthly churn. Industry churn commonly runs four to six percent a month, and the gyms that keep it near two to three earn multiples more per member at the exact same price. That is why pouring leads into a gym with a retention problem mostly just raises your ad spend.

January fills the room and February empties it

Resolution joiners leave fast, and a large share are gone within weeks. Meanwhile November and December quietly carry the highest net member loss of the year. The gyms that survive this plan cash around it instead of being surprised by it every single year, and they use January to fill an on-ramp rather than to sell twelve-month contracts to people who will ghost.

A ninety-nine dollar special buys you the wrong member

Discount pricing attracts a price shopper, and a price shopper churns. If your model depends on coaching, small groups, and high touch, your margin comes from members who stay a long time, not from filling a six a.m. class with people who disappear by week three. The offer should filter for fit, not just for volume.

Half the people you want are afraid to walk in

Surveys of non-members consistently find that a large share find the idea of a gym intimidating, and many believe they need to get in shape before they are allowed to start. If your content only shows your fittest members at their best, you are confirming that fear. Showing real members who look like the person on the couch is what gets them through the door.
