How Do I Remove a Fake Google Review?

J
Jayson Hines August 28, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: open your Google Business Profile, find the review, click the three dots on it and report it. Then go into Google's Reviews Management Tool, which is where you can actually see the status of what you reported and file an appeal if they say no. Screenshot everything before you do any of it.

Now the part nobody wants to hear. Google will not take a review down because it's untrue. It takes reviews down when they break a content policy. Those are two very different things, and if you file your report saying "this never happened, it's a lie," you're probably getting a rejection. You have to point at the actual rule it breaks.

What kind of reviews will Google actually remove?

The categories that matter for a local business are pretty short.

Spam and fake engagement, meaning a review from somebody who was never a customer, or one posted by a bot or a paid account. Conflict of interest, which covers competitors and former employees leaving reviews about you. Off topic, where somebody rants about something that has nothing to do with your business, like the traffic on the road outside. Then the obvious stuff: profanity, harassment, hate speech, anything sexually explicit, and impersonation.

What isn't on that list is "the customer got the story wrong" or "they're exaggerating" or "we bent over backwards for this guy." A one star from a real person who had a genuinely bad day at your shop is going to stay up, and it should. That one you answer instead of fight.

So before you file anything, read the review again and pick your lane. Which of those rules is it breaking? That sentence is your whole report.

How do I actually report it?

Find the review in your profile, either in the Google app, in Search when you're signed in as the owner, or in Maps. There's a three dot menu on the review itself. Report review. Pick the reason that matches the policy you landed on above, and be specific in the box.

Then go find the Reviews Management Tool. Google runs it as a separate support page and it's genuinely worth using instead of just flagging and hoping. It shows you what you've reported, where it stands, and it's the door to the appeal if the first answer is no. Half the owners I talk to have never heard of it and have been flagging reviews into a void for years.

Before any of that, take your screenshots. The review, the reviewer's profile page, the date, and the other reviews that account has left. Profiles change and accounts get deleted, and if you end up appealing in three weeks you'll want the evidence you had on day one.

How do I prove somebody was never a customer?

Go look it up. This is the strongest thing you can put in an appeal and most people skip it.

Pull the name from the review and run it through your point of sale, your CRM, your appointment book, your invoices, your call log. If there's no record of that person ever buying anything or booking anything, say exactly that in the report. "We searched our customer records from the last three years and have no transaction, appointment, or contact matching this name" is worlds better than "this is fake."

Then look at the reviewer's account. Brand new profile with no photo? One review ever, and it's yours? Or the opposite, forty reviews in three days across a dozen cities? Reviewed four of your direct competitors and gave them all five stars? Write it down. That pattern is the argument.

What do I do while I'm waiting?

Reply to it. Publicly, calmly, once.

This one matters more than the removal does, because the removal might never come and the reply is visible to every person who reads that review in the meantime. Keep it short. Say you have no record of them as a customer, say you'd genuinely like to sort it out if you've missed something, and give them a way to reach you. That's it.

Do not argue. Do not get sarcastic, do not mention the competitor you think is behind it, and never post anything about their personal details. The audience for that reply is not the person who wrote the review. It's the woman reading your profile next Tuesday trying to decide whether to call you, and a calm owner reply next to a wild accusation tells her everything she needs to know.

What if Google says it doesn't violate policy?

You get an appeal. Generally one, so don't waste it by resubmitting the same complaint with more feeling.

Add something new. The customer record search you did. The screenshots of the reviewer's other activity. A timeline if there was a burst of them. Name the specific policy again. If your first report just said "fake review" and your appeal says "no matching record in our system, the account was created the same week, and it has reviewed three of our direct competitors positively," that's a different case entirely.

Expect it to take a while. A few days to a couple of weeks on the first pass is what most owners report, and an appeal can add another week. Reporting the same review over and over doesn't speed anything up, it just makes a messier file.

What if I'm getting hit with a pile of them at once?

Report every single one separately. There's no bulk button, and lumping them into one complaint gets you one decision instead of ten.

But build the pattern while you do it. Note the dates and times, the account ages, and anything the reviews have in common, like the same odd phrasing showing up in four of them. Then contact Google Business Profile support directly with that timeline attached. A coordinated attack is a much easier thing for a human reviewer to see than one weird review on its own.

Also, go check your profile for other damage while you're in there. Review bombs sometimes come alongside edits to your hours, your category, or your address. And if the whole profile goes dark on you, that's a separate mess I covered in why your Google Business Profile got suspended.

Is it worth getting a lawyer involved?

Sometimes, and I want to be careful here because I'm not a lawyer and none of this is legal advice.

Business owners do take fake reviews to small claims, and a letter from an attorney sometimes gets a review pulled down faster than anything Google will do. Google will also act on a court order. So it's a real path.

It's also slow, it costs money, and there's a risk people don't think about, which is that suing over a review can turn something twelve people saw into something the whole town hears about. Weigh that honestly before you start. If the review is accusing you of something that could genuinely cost you your business, go talk to an actual attorney. If it's a nasty one star that stung, save your money.

What actually protects you from this?

Volume. It's boring and it works.

One fake one star sitting under nine real reviews is a disaster. That same review under two hundred and forty is a rounding error that most people scroll right past. The businesses that panic about a single bad review are almost always the ones who never built a habit of asking for the good ones, so every review carries way too much weight.

So while you're waiting on Google, go ask ten happy customers this week. I put the whole system for that in how to get more Google reviews for your business, and it's the single highest return thing a local business can do with twenty minutes.

Common questions

How long does it take Google to remove a fake review?
Most people report hearing back somewhere between a few days and a couple of weeks after they flag it, and an appeal can add another week on top of that. There's no status bar and no rep to call. If you've heard nothing after two weeks, go into the Reviews Management Tool and check the status there rather than flagging it a second time.

Can I get a review removed just because it isn't true?
No, and this is the part that makes owners crazy. Google isn't judging whether the customer was right. It's checking whether the review breaks a content policy. An unfair one star from a real customer who had a bad day stays up. Your move there is a good public reply, not a removal request.

Should I ask the person to take it down themselves?
If it's a real customer, yes, and it's often the fastest fix. Sort the actual problem out first, then ask. A reviewer can delete or edit their own review in seconds, which is quicker than anything Google will do for you. Never offer money or a discount in exchange, that breaks the rules and can get you in worse trouble.

What if a competitor is leaving fake reviews?
That's a conflict of interest under Google's policies and it's one of the clearer cases for removal. Document it. Screenshot the review and the reviewer's profile, look at what else that account has posted, and note if they've reviewed several of your competitors positively. Then report it and include what you found in the appeal.

Can I sue somebody over a fake Google review?
People do, usually in small claims, and sometimes just having a lawyer send a letter is enough to make the review disappear. But it's slow, it costs money, and it can turn a review nobody noticed into a story people talk about. I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice, so if the review is genuinely damaging, go talk to a real one before you decide.
