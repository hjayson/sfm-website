Why Does Facebook Say My Post Goes Against Community Standards?

J
Jayson Hines September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: Facebook removed your post because an automated system, not a person, matched it against one of Meta's Community Standards rules. Someone reporting you is not required. Most of the time it's the classifier misreading a word, an image, or a link, and you can appeal it from the notification itself. If the appeal works the post comes back and the strike goes away.

I get this call from business owners more than almost anything else. A pest control guy posts a before and after photo and Facebook pulls it. A gun shop posts store hours and gets a strike. A med spa posts a client result and the whole thing vanishes with a scary red notice. Then they sit there wondering if their page is about to get deleted.

Usually it isn't. But you do need to handle it correctly, because the strikes stack.

Does it mean somebody reported my post?

No, and this is the part people get wrong. Meta's own enforcement numbers show the overwhelming majority of content removals start with automated detection, before any human report exists. A model scanned your text, your image, and your link, decided it looked like something on the banned list, and pulled it.

Reports do exist and a competitor can absolutely report you out of spite. But a report by itself doesn't remove anything. It just puts your post in a review queue. So if your post came down two minutes after you hit publish, that was a machine, not a neighbor.

Why did a totally normal business post get flagged?

Because the classifier doesn't understand context. Here are the ones I actually see from local businesses.

Words that mean something else somewhere else. "Shoot me a message." "Killer deal." "This treatment is a weapon against wrinkles." Restoration companies and pest control get hit constantly because the vocabulary of their trade overlaps with violence and weapons policy.

Skin in the photo. Med spas, tattoo shops, gyms, and chiropractors all run into this. Before and after body photos trip the adult content and the health claims filters at the same time. Cropping tighter usually fixes it.

Health and money promises. Anything that reads like a guaranteed result. Lose 20 pounds. Fix your credit. Cure your back pain. Those phrases are explicitly restricted and the system doesn't care that you're a legitimate clinic.

A link the system doesn't trust. Shortened links, a brand new domain, or a landing page somebody else already got flagged for. Your post is fine and the URL is the problem.

Recycled content. Copying the same caption across a dozen pages, or reposting something that was removed on another account, gets read as coordinated spam.

None of that is you being a bad actor. It's pattern matching at a scale where they accept a certain amount of collateral damage, and small local pages are the collateral.

How do I appeal it and does appealing actually work?

Appeal it. Every time. Even when you think it's hopeless.

Go to the notification Facebook sent you, or open Account Status under Professional Dashboard on your page. You'll see the removed content, which policy it supposedly broke, and a button that says you disagree. Tap it, give one or two plain sentences of context, and submit.

Meta says that when a review finds your content did follow the standards, they restore the post and wipe the strike so it doesn't count later. That last part is the reason to always appeal even if you no longer care about the post. You're clearing the strike off your record, not saving the caption.

Keep the explanation short and boring. "This is a photo of a completed roof repair on a customer's home. There is no violent or adult content in it." Don't argue about free speech, don't mention how long you've been a customer, don't be clever. A tired reviewer or a second model is reading it.

If the appeal comes back denied and you're certain it's wrong, you can escalate to the Oversight Board through the same flow for eligible cases. That's slow and most business posts won't qualify, so don't wait on it. Repost a cleaned-up version instead.

How many strikes before Facebook restricts my page?

Meta runs a strike count. Most strikes drop off after 90 days and the severe ones stick around for years.

A first violation is usually just a warning with no restriction. From there it escalates. A handful of strikes gets you short holds on posting, going live, or running ads, in the range of one to seven days. Ten or more and you're looking at a 30 day block on creating content, and at that point removal of the page is on the table.

So the danger isn't the one post. It's posting the same kind of thing four more times because you didn't realize what got flagged, and stacking strikes in a single week. Read the violation notice before you post again.

What should I do right now?

Do these in order.

First, appeal the removal. Second, open Account Status and look at your full history so you know how many strikes you're carrying and when they expire. Third, find the specific thing in the post that matched, and it's usually a single word, one image, or the link. Fourth, repost without that element and see if it goes through.

If you run ads, check whether your ad account got restricted too, because page level strikes bleed into ad delivery. I wrote separately about what to do when Facebook rejects your ad and when your page gets unpublished, and both of those start the same way this one does.

And then stop building your business on rented land. Every owner I've had this conversation with is one algorithm mistake away from losing their whole customer list, because the list lives inside Facebook. Get an email list going. Get your Google Business Profile solid. Run your traffic to a site you actually own. That's most of what we do for clients on the social media management side, and it's the part that keeps a page removal from being a business emergency.

One more thing. Screenshot the violation notice before you appeal. Facebook clears those notifications and if you ever need to explain your history to support, the screenshot is all you'll have.

Common questions

Does a Facebook community standards removal mean someone reported me?
Usually not. Most removals come from automated detection that scans your post the moment you publish it, before any person sees it. Reports do exist, but a report on its own only sends your post to a review queue. If your post came down within a couple of minutes, that was a machine.

Should I appeal if Facebook removed my post?
Yes, every time. If the review finds your content actually followed the standards, Meta restores the post and removes the strike so it will not count against you later. Even if you no longer want the post up, appealing is how you clear the strike off your record.

How many strikes before Facebook restricts my business page?
A first violation is normally a warning with no restriction. After that it escalates into short holds on posting, going live, or running ads. Around ten strikes you are looking at a 30 day block on creating content, and page removal becomes possible. Most strikes expire after 90 days.

Why do normal business posts get flagged as violations?
The classifier reads words and images without context. Trade vocabulary like shoot, killer, or weapon trips the violence filters. Before and after body photos trip adult content and health claim filters. Guaranteed result language and untrusted or shortened links do it too.

Can I just delete the post and move on?
You can, but deleting it does not remove the strike. The violation stays on your record either way, so appeal first and delete after. Also screenshot the violation notice before you appeal, because Facebook clears those notifications and you will want the record later.
