Why Is My LinkedIn Post Getting No Views?

J
Jayson Hines September 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: your post probably isn't broken. LinkedIn is showing it to a much smaller test group than it used to, and if that group doesn't stop and read it in the first few hours, distribution quits there. Add an outbound link in the body of the post, or post from a company page instead of your personal profile, and you shrink that test group even further. Reach on LinkedIn is down sharply across the board since 2023, so a post that would have pulled 2,000 impressions two years ago might pull a few hundred now with nothing wrong with it.

That's the honest version. Now here's what's actually happening under the hood and what you can change.

What does "no views" actually mean on LinkedIn?

First thing worth sorting out, because people mean two different things by it.

Impressions means your post showed up in somebody's feed. Views on a video means somebody watched it. Members reached is the unique-people count. If you post something and see 40 impressions, that's not zero, that's LinkedIn testing you with a small slice of your network and then stopping.

Actual zero is rare and usually means something else went wrong, like the post failed to publish, or you posted it to a group nobody's in, or your account got restricted. Check your profile in a private browser window. If the post is there and visible to a logged-out visitor, it published fine and this is a distribution problem, not a bug.

Why did my LinkedIn reach drop so much?

Because LinkedIn changed what it's optimizing for and most people didn't notice.

It used to work off your connections. You posted, your network saw it, and if enough of them liked it fast, it spilled over to their networks. That's mostly gone. LinkedIn now reads what your post is actually about and tries to match it to people who care about that topic, whether or not they know you. Reports across the platform put average impressions per post down somewhere in the 60 percent range since 2023.

Here's the part that surprises people though. Engagement per post went up over that same stretch. Fewer people are seeing your stuff, but the ones who do are more likely to be the right ones. So if you're judging your LinkedIn by impressions alone you're going to feel like you're failing even when you're not. I'd rather have 200 impressions from contractors and business owners than 2,000 from strangers who will never call.

Does putting a link in my post kill my reach?

Yes, and this is the single most common thing I see local business owners doing wrong.

Drop a link to your website in the body of a LinkedIn post and you can lose a big chunk of your reach, some estimates run as high as 60 percent. LinkedIn wants people staying on LinkedIn. The old trick was to put the link in the first comment instead. That worked for a while. It doesn't really work anymore, comments with external links get suppressed too.

So what do you do if you actually want traffic? Write the post so it stands on its own, give the whole point away in the post itself, and then say "link's in my profile" or tell people to comment a word and you'll send it. Fewer clicks, way more people seeing it. Or just accept that some posts are for reach and some are for clicks and stop trying to make one post do both.

What happens in the first few hours after I post?

LinkedIn shows your post to a small sample and watches what they do. People used to call this the golden hour. It's a little longer than that now, more like the first several hours, but the idea holds.

What it's watching for isn't likes. Comments carry far more weight than a like does, and a real comment with a sentence in it counts more than "Great post!" The other thing it measures is dwell time, meaning how long somebody's thumb stops on your post before they keep scrolling. Roughly half a minute of attention is a strong signal. That's why a post with a good first line and some actual substance beats a clever one-liner, even though the one-liner feels punchier.

Practical version: post when your people are actually online, and then stay on LinkedIn for the next 30 minutes answering comments. Every reply you leave is another signal. Ghosting your own post right after you hit publish is the cheapest way to kill it.

Is it my content, or is it me?

Little of both usually.

The stuff that gets buried is easy to spot once you know the pattern. Pure promotion, and anything that reads like it came out of a press release. LinkedIn's models have gotten good at recognizing broadcast content and quietly declining to distribute it. If your post could have been written by any company in your industry, that's the problem.

The other half is you. If you post twice a month and never comment on anybody else's stuff, LinkedIn has no reason to think anyone wants to hear from you. The people whose posts do numbers are almost always the people spending 15 minutes a day in other people's comment sections. That's not a growth hack, it's just how the thing works.

One more: hooks. Everyone sees your first line and a "see more" button. If line one is a throat-clear like "I'm excited to share that our team recently attended," nobody's expanding it, dwell time stays at two seconds, and the post dies. Put the interesting part first.

Why do my company page posts get almost nothing?

Because company pages got hit harder than personal profiles did. A lot harder. Some estimates put company page reach down 60 percent or more.

LinkedIn is a network of people and it behaves like one. A post from Jayson Hines will outperform the same post from a logo almost every time. For most local businesses I work with, the answer is to post from the owner's personal profile and use the company page as a credibility page that people check after they've already met you.

If your team will play along, have a few employees post in their own voices about the work. That beats anything the page will do on its own. And if you want LinkedIn generating actual conversations instead of just impressions, I went deeper on that in how to generate leads on LinkedIn.

What I'd actually change this week

Skip the overhaul. Do these and check back in three weeks.

Take the links out of your post bodies. Rewrite your first line so it earns the click on "see more." Post from your own profile instead of the page. Block 15 minutes a day for commenting on other people's posts, real comments, not emoji. And stop tracking impressions as your scoreboard, track how many conversations came out of it.

If you've done all that and LinkedIn still isn't returning anything, it might just be the wrong room for your business. Plenty of local trades and retail shops get more out of Facebook and Google than they'll ever get out of LinkedIn. That's a strategy question, and it's the kind of thing we sort out on a call when we build out a client's social media marketing plan. There's no rule that says you have to win on every platform.

Common questions

Why is my LinkedIn post getting no views?
Most of the time it did get views, just far fewer than you expected. LinkedIn shows a new post to a small test group first, and if that group does not comment or spend real time on it, distribution stops there. Outbound links in the post body and posting from a company page both shrink that test group further.

Does LinkedIn punish posts with links in them?
Yes. Putting an external link in the body of a post can cost a large share of your reach, and dropping it in the first comment instead no longer gets around it. Write the post so it stands alone and point people to your profile if they want the link.

How long does LinkedIn test a post before it stops showing it?
The early window is a few hours, not the strict 60 minutes people used to talk about. Comments and dwell time during that window matter far more than likes, so replying to every comment in the first half hour genuinely helps.

Why do my company page posts get less reach than my personal posts?
Company pages took the biggest hit when LinkedIn changed how it distributes content, with reach down more than 60 percent by most estimates. For local businesses the owner's personal profile almost always outperforms the logo, so post there and let the page serve as a credibility check.

Should my local business even be on LinkedIn?
It depends on who buys from you. If you sell to other businesses or to professionals, yes. If you are a trade or a retail shop selling to homeowners, Facebook and Google usually return more for the same effort, and there is no rule that you have to win on every platform.
