# TikTok Is a Powerhouse. Here's What Actually Holds Up.

*We went through the primary sources for client work. The case is stronger than most local owners think, and weaker in a few spots than the marketing internet will tell you.*

We spent a couple of days this month going through TikTok's real numbers for client work. Not the stat-blog version. Earnings calls, Pew, the Census Bureau, and research reports that disclose their methodology.

Short version: TikTok is a bigger deal than a lot of business owners around here think. The case for it is stronger in some places and thinner in others than you'd guess from what circulates on LinkedIn.

Here's what held up.

## The commerce number is the real story

[TikTok Shop did an estimated $15.1 billion in U.S. sales in 2025](https://thelowdown.momentum.asia/new-report-tiktok-shop-u-s-gmv-grew-68-to-reach-us15-1b-in-2025/), up 68% from $9 billion the year before. [The first half of 2026 alone came in around $11.8 billion](https://momentum.asia/insights/detail/tiktok-shop-in-the-us-h1-2026), roughly double the same stretch last year.

That comes from Momentum Works, a research firm that tracks this with a data partner. It's one vendor's estimate rather than an audited figure, so hold it a little loosely. But very little in U.S. retail is growing at that rate, and no competing estimate contradicts it by much.

If you sell a physical product somebody can buy in the next thirty seconds, that's a real channel with real money moving through it.

## It stopped being a teenager app a while ago

This is the part I had wrong in my own head.

[Pew surveyed 5,022 U.S. adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/11/20/americans-social-media-use-2025/) last year and published in November. TikTok sits at 37% of all American adults, up from 21% in 2021. Facebook and YouTube stayed flat across that same stretch.

[By age](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/): 63% of 18- to 29-year-olds, 44% of 30- to 49-year-olds, 30% of 50- to 64-year-olds, and 12% of those 65 and up.

That 44% is the one that got my attention. Nearly half of people in their thirties and forties are on TikTok. That's the bracket buying houses, cars, braces, and kitchen remodels.

## Gen Z finds restaurants there first

[Toast surveyed U.S. diners](https://pos.toasttab.com/th-us/blog/data/how-guests-discover-new-restaurants) in the fall of 2025. TikTok drives 38% of Gen Z restaurant discovery, the single biggest channel, ahead of word of mouth.

Toast sells restaurant point-of-sale systems, so they've got an interest here, and their own footnotes disagree with their body copy on the sample size. Worth knowing. It's still better sourced than most of what gets quoted in this space, and I couldn't find anything contradicting it.

If you own a restaurant and you're not making short video, that's the clearest case in this whole pile.

## New accounts genuinely get a fair shot

[TikTok's recommendation system](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en) doesn't use follower count as a direct ranking factor. That's from their own engineering explainer, though the post dates to 2020 and it's a company describing its own product, so weigh it accordingly.

Taken at face value, a brand-new account's video enters roughly the same distribution test as an established account's. Facebook and Instagram lean harder on who already follows you.

So a business starting from zero can get seen faster on TikTok. That isn't a myth. It's a real difference in how the two systems are built.

## The ban question is settled

This still comes up on nearly every sales call, so let's put it down. [The TikTok U.S. joint venture](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/announcement-from-the-new-tiktok-usds-joint-venture-llc?lang=en) closed in January 2026. Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX each hold 15%, and ByteDance holds 19.9%.

If somebody's telling you not to build there because it might vanish, that argument expired.

## Now the parts that don't get quoted

I'd rather you hear these from me than run into them later.

That same Momentum Works report counted 803,500 U.S. TikTok Shop stores and found more than half had recorded zero sales. Just over 2,000 cleared a million dollars. So the channel is enormous and most people who open a store on it sell nothing at all.

Both of those are true at the same time, and anybody quoting you the first number without the second one is selling you something.

There's no credible independent study comparing TikTok ad costs with Meta. Every CPM and cost-per-lead figure in circulation traces back to agency blogs with no disclosed sample size or methodology. The cost advantage might well be real. Nobody has published proof of it.

I also went looking for a documented case of a local home services company, an HVAC or roofing outfit, pulling measurable customers off TikTok. Every example I found traced back to an agency selling TikTok services to contractors. No client names, no verifiable numbers.

And for high-ticket home services specifically, the demographics still point somewhere else first. [Census data](https://www.census.gov/housing/hvs/current/) puts homeownership in the mid-thirties for under-35s, climbing every decade after that. Facebook and YouTube remain the only two platforms a majority of every age group uses. Among 30- to 49-year-olds, 58% open Facebook daily, against 24% daily TikTok use across all adults.

TikTok reaches your middle-aged customers. Facebook reaches them more often.

## One note on how we got here

While putting this together, I nearly left the 44% figure out entirely.

I went to Pew's report to cite it, found the age breakdown locked inside a chart image rather than published as text, couldn't verify it, and cut it. A fact-check pass then found it in about a minute, sitting on Pew's Social Media Fact Sheet, from the identical survey, published as plain text.

I'd stopped looking after the first place I checked. And the number I dropped happened to be the single strongest one in TikTok's favor.

That's worth saying out loud because it's how most bad marketing advice gets made. It doesn't take dishonesty. Somebody finds a number that fits what they already believe, it sounds about right, and they don't push any further.

The bias usually isn't in the checking. It's in deciding which claims get checked.

## What to actually do with this

If you sell food, drinks, beauty, fitness, or a physical product, TikTok deserves real effort and probably more than you're currently giving it. The discovery behavior is documented and the commerce side is built out.

If you sell roofs or furnaces or estate plans, get your Google Business Profile, your reviews, and Facebook working first. Then treat TikTok as a serious second channel instead of a novelty, because 44% of your buying-age customers are already on it.

Whatever you run, put a tracking number or a form on it. Most of the local businesses I talk to can't tell me where their last ten customers came from, and that's a bigger problem than picking the wrong app.

One last thing. If somebody hands you a deck full of percentages, ask where each number came from and what year it's from. A good partner will tell you, and will say "I don't know" when that's the honest answer.
