Should My Small Business Be on TikTok?

J
Jayson Hines August 25, 2026 · 9 min read

Short answer: probably not first, and it depends almost entirely on how old your customer is. If you sell food, drinks, beauty services, or a physical product people can buy on impulse, TikTok has a real case and you should take it seriously. If you replace roofs or furnaces, do estate planning, or install kitchens, the person signing that check is closer to 60 than 30, and your money works harder on Facebook, YouTube, and Google first. That's not a hunch. It's Census data next to Pew data, and I'll show you both.

I got pulled into this because a chart went around this week ranking daily video watch time by platform. TikTok on top at 1.4 billion hours, YouTube at 1 billion, Meta at 100 million, Snapchat at 3.5 million. The caption said TikTok is the new king of video and if you're ignoring it you're making a big mistake.

So I went and checked all four numbers. What I found was worse than a wrong chart, and it changed what I think this whole argument is even about.

Why can't anyone agree on how much video people watch?

Because three of the four biggest video platforms have never published the number.

Start with Meta, because that bar is the most obviously broken. The chart put Meta at 100 million hours of video a day. On Meta's own earnings call on October 29th, Zuckerberg said Reels get played more than 200 billion times a day across Facebook and Instagram. Even if you assume every single play lasted 10 seconds, that's over 500 million hours a day from Reels alone. Meta reported 3.6 billion daily active people across its apps last quarter. A hundred million hours spread across 3.6 billion people works out to under two minutes of video each per day. That's not a rounding error, it's off by a factor of ten or more.

Snapchat at 3.5 million hours has the same problem. Snap reported 493 million daily active users in its most recent quarter. Divide it out and you get about 25 seconds of video per user per day.

YouTube's 1 billion hours is a real number. It's also from February 28, 2017. It came from YouTube's own blog, posted by their VP of engineering, and as far as I can tell they've never publicly updated it since. Nine years. YouTube Shorts alone now does 200 billion views a day according to their CEO's most recent letter, so using the 2017 figure badly understates them.

TikTok's 1.4 billion is the only bar in the plausible range. It's also unverifiable, because TikTok has never published watch time at all.

Which brings me to the actual finding here. YouTube said it once in 2017 and went quiet. Meta reports plays, not hours. TikTok publishes nothing. Snap reports users, not video hours. Three of the four companies on that chart have never released the metric the chart was ranking them on. Nobody did bad math. The numbers just don't exist, and somebody who needed four bars went shopping across five stat-aggregator blogs to fill them in.

How do I know if a marketing stat is any good?

Ask two questions. Who originally published it, and when.

That sounds obvious. It caught me anyway, on this exact article, and I'd rather tell you than pretend it didn't.

I was building the counter-argument to that chart and I had a stat lined up: 44% of adults aged 30 to 49 use TikTok. Good number for my case, made TikTok look more mainstream than people assume, showed I wasn't cherry-picking. Before publishing I went to the Pew Research page to cite it properly. It wasn't there. I'd picked it up from a search summary that was summarizing somebody else's summary. Pew's actual age breakdown for TikTok lives inside a chart image, not in the text, and I could not verify that figure. So I cut it, and you won't find it anywhere in this post.

I came within about ten minutes of doing the exact thing I'd spent all morning criticizing somebody else for. That's how this happens. Nobody sits down to publish a fake statistic. You just find a number that supports what you already believe and you don't check it hard enough.

Here's a short list of things that get repeated constantly in marketing and do not hold up:

"40% of young people use TikTok instead of Google." This traces to a Google executive speaking at a conference on July 12, 2022. He said "something like almost 40%," he was talking about TikTok and Instagram combined, the split was never disclosed, it covered 18 to 24 year olds only, and it was specifically about finding a place to eat lunch. Google said the data might be published later. It never was.

"46% of Google searches have local intent." An unnamed Google employee said this at a conference in 2018. Google never published it as research, never defined what "local intent" means, and never updated it. It's eight years old and it's in half the local SEO decks in America.

"TikTok ads are way cheaper than Meta ads." There is no credible independent study supporting any specific number here. Every figure in circulation comes from agency blogs with no disclosed sample size or methodology. The cost difference might be real. Nobody has published proof.

If you're paying an agency and they hand you a deck full of percentages, ask where each one came from. A good one will tell you. If you want to know what to expect from a real one, I put together an honest breakdown of what social media marketing actually costs around here.

Is TikTok actually worth it for a small business?

For some businesses, yes, and I want to make that case properly rather than wave it off.

TikTok Shop did an estimated $15.1 billion in US sales in 2025, up 68% from the year before, and roughly $11.8 billion in the first half of 2026 alone. That estimate comes from Momentum Works, a research firm, and it's one vendor's number rather than an audited figure, so hold it loosely. But nothing else in US retail is growing at that rate. If you sell a physical product someone can buy in the next thirty seconds, that is a real channel and ignoring it is a genuine mistake.

Restaurants are the other clear case. Toast surveyed 1,466 US diners in October 2025 and found TikTok drives 38% of Gen Z restaurant discovery, the single biggest channel, ahead of word of mouth. Nearly half of diners aged 18 to 34 use social media to find a new place to eat, compared to about a third of people over 35. Toast sells restaurant point-of-sale systems so they have a horse in the race, but they published their sample size and field dates, which puts them ahead of most of what gets quoted in this space.

There's a structural argument too. TikTok's recommendation system doesn't use follower count as a direct ranking factor, which they've said publicly in their own engineering explainer. A brand new account's video enters roughly the same distribution test as a big account's. Facebook and Instagram lean much harder on your existing social graph. So a business starting from zero genuinely can get seen faster on TikTok, and that's not a myth, it's how the two systems are built differently.

And TikTok is growing here. Pew has US adult usage going from 21% in 2021 to 37% in 2025, while Facebook and YouTube stayed flat. Anyone telling you TikTok is a teenager thing is working from a 2020 picture.

One more thing worth saying plainly, because it comes up on every sales call: the ban question is over. The TikTok US joint venture closed on January 22, 2026, with Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX each holding around 15% and ByteDance under 20%. If someone is still telling you not to build on TikTok because it might disappear, that argument expired a year ago.

So where should a local business actually spend?

Here's where the demographics stop being an opinion.

Pew surveyed 5,022 US adults between February and June of 2025 and published in November. YouTube reaches 84% of American adults. Facebook 71%. Instagram 50%. TikTok 37%. The line that matters most for a local business is this one, straight from the report: YouTube and Facebook are the only platforms a majority of every age group uses. Facebook peaks among 30 to 49 year olds at 80% of them.

Daily use tells you even more. About half of American adults open Facebook and YouTube every single day. For TikTok it's 24%. Among 30 to 49 year olds, 58% are on Facebook daily.

Now put that next to who actually buys expensive home services. The Census Bureau's housing survey from July 2026 puts the homeownership rate at 35.2% for people under 35 and 78.6% for people 65 and over. The National Association of Realtors reported in November 2025 that the median age of an American home buyer hit 59, an all-time high, with repeat buyers at 62.

Sit with that for a second. The median person buying a house in this country is 59 years old. They are the one calling about a new roof, a furnace, a bathroom remodel, or a will. On TikTok, that person is a rounding error. On Facebook and YouTube, they're most of the audience.

That's the whole answer, and it has nothing to do with which app has more global watch hours. A platform can be the biggest thing on earth and still be the wrong place for your business, because you don't have a global audience. You have a service area that's maybe forty miles wide.

What should I do this week?

If you sell food, drinks, beauty, fitness, or a physical product, put real effort into short video and treat TikTok and Reels as a serious channel. Your buyer is young enough and the discovery behavior is documented.

If you sell high-ticket home services, professional services, or anything a 55 year old buys, get your Google Business Profile and reviews right first, because that's where people check you out before they call. Then Facebook, because your buyer opens it daily. Then YouTube, which is worth more than people think for local businesses. Nielsen has YouTube as the single largest distributor of US television viewing for eleven straight months, hitting a record 13.8% of all American TV time in May 2026. It's the only television buy a local contractor can actually afford, and you can draw a circle around your service area.

Whatever you pick, put a tracking number or a form on it so you know what worked. That matters more than the platform. Most of the local businesses I talk to can't tell me where their last ten customers came from, and that's a bigger problem than being on the wrong app. If you want help sorting out which channels are worth your money, that's most of what we do when we build a marketing system for a local business.

And one honest admission to close on. There is no good published data on where local service business leads actually come from. I went looking for a representative survey of home services lead sources and there isn't one. Every percentage you've seen on that is unsourceable. So anyone who tells you with total confidence that 60% of contractor leads come from any single channel is guessing, including the people who agree with me.

Common questions

Is TikTok bigger than YouTube?
Nobody can answer that with published data, because YouTube last released daily watch hours in February 2017 and TikTok has never released the figure at all. On US reach, Pew has YouTube at 84% of adults and TikTok at 37%. On time per user, most estimates put TikTok ahead. They measure different things and both are partly estimates.

Should a plumber or HVAC company be on TikTok?
Not as a first move. Homeownership runs 35% for under-35s and 79% for over-65s per Census data, and the median US home buyer is 59. Your buyer is concentrated where TikTok is weakest. Google Business Profile, reviews, and Facebook will do more per dollar. TikTok is a fine experiment once those are working.

Which platform has the best organic reach for a new business account?
TikTok, structurally. Their recommendation system doesn't treat follower count as a direct ranking factor, so a new account's video gets tested on roughly the same footing as an established one. Instagram and Facebook lean more on your existing followers. Just remember reach is not the same as revenue.

How do I tell if a marketing statistic is trustworthy?
Ask who originally published it and what year. If the trail leads to a stat-aggregator blog citing another stat-aggregator blog, it isn't a source. Company earnings calls, SEC filings, Pew Research, and the Census Bureau are. A date older than about three years on a platform statistic usually means it's stale.

Can I just ignore TikTok if my customers are older?
For now, mostly yes, but check back yearly. TikTok went from 21% to 37% of US adults between 2021 and 2025 while Facebook and YouTube stayed flat. The age gap that makes it skippable today is narrowing every year, so this is a decision to revisit, not settle.
